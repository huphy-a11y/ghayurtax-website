Should I incorporate?

It's a numbers question before it's a paperwork question.

A corporation is a separate taxpayer. In Ontario it pays about 11.2% on its first $500,000 of active business income (the Ontario small-business rate fell from 3.2% to 2.2% on July 1, 2026); the top personal rate is 53.53%. That gap is only useful if you can leave money in the corporation — if you spend everything the business earns, incorporating mostly adds cost.

Reasons it usually makes sense

  • You earn more than you need to live on. Retained income is taxed at the small business rate and invested or held inside the corporation.
  • You want to separate the business from yourself. Contracts, leases and debts sit with the corporation. (Professional negligence and personally guaranteed loans still follow you.)
  • Customers or contracts require it. Some clients and agencies only deal with incorporated suppliers.
  • You're planning for a sale, a partner, or family involvement. Shares can be structured for it from day one.

Reasons to wait

  • The business is small or part-time and you spend what it earns.
  • You expect losses in the first year or two — inside a corporation they can't offset your other personal income.
  • You'd be incorporating to split income with family: the tax on split income rules have narrowed that considerably since 2018.

Physicians and other regulated professionals have their own rules — see why and when a physician should incorporate, including with student loans.

Weighing it up? Should you incorporate your business in Ontario? works through the arithmetic, the cases where it does not pay, and what a corporation costs to run each year.

Ontario, federal or another province?

Ontario — the usual choice for a business operating in Ontario. Filed through the Ontario Business Registry; the name is protected in Ontario.

Federal — name protected across Canada; a little more annual paperwork, and the corporation still registers in Ontario to operate here. Worth it if you'll trade under the name in other provinces.

Another province — if the business will be based outside Ontario, we prepare and file there too; the registry and the fees differ by province and are quoted before we start.

Government filing fees are $300 for Ontario and $200 federally — see what it costs.

Already a sole proprietor?

The business, its equipment and its goodwill can usually move into the new corporation on a tax-deferred basis with the right election. We plan the transfer so the switch doesn't trigger tax.

What we do

From the name search to the first payroll run.

The steps below are the sequence we follow for every incorporation. Most Ontario filings are complete within a few business days of the decision.

  1. Decide, with your numbers

    Income, spending, debt and plans on one page. If incorporating doesn't pay for itself, we'll say so.

  2. Name or number

    A numbered corporation is fastest and can carry a business name. For a named corporation we run the NUANS name search and check the name is available.

  3. Articles and share structure

    Share classes that fit what you're planning — an owner-operator, a spouse or family member, a future partner, or the specific share provisions a professional corporation has to have.

  4. File and register

    Articles filed with Ontario or Corporations Canada, the Ontario registration for a federal corporation, and the CRA business number with corporate tax, HST and payroll accounts opened as needed.

  5. Minute book and organization

    By-laws, organizational resolutions, share certificates and the registers, kept up to date each year with the annual resolutions and the Ontario annual return.

  6. Set up the books and the year-end

    Books set up with a chart of accounts built for the business, a December 31 year-end so the corporation and your personal return run on one calendar, and a first-year plan for salary and dividends.

  7. Professional corporations

    For physicians, dentists and other regulated professionals we prepare the articles to the regulator's requirements and give you the incorporation documents your College asks for. The Certificate of Authorization application itself you file with the College — we don't do that part.

After incorporation

What changes on day one.

  • A separate bank account for the corporation — no mixing with personal money.
  • Invoices, contracts and leases in the corporation's name.
  • HST registration once sales pass $30,000 in four consecutive quarters (or earlier by choice).
  • Payroll if you or anyone else takes a salary — remittances on the 15th, T4s by the end of February.
  • Deadlines of its own: the T2 six months after year-end, the balance two or three months after, the Ontario annual return, and instalments once tax passes $3,000. The Deadline tools page shows yours.
  • Your personal return still matters. Salary and dividends come out to you and are taxed on your T1 — which is why we prepare both together.

Common questions

What people ask before incorporating.

Do I need a lawyer to incorporate?

Not for a standard incorporation. We prepare and file the articles, open the CRA accounts and, if you want one, set up the minute book. If you need a shareholder agreement between unrelated partners, or legal advice on a dispute or a complex ownership structure, that is a lawyer's work and we'll tell you so.

How much does it cost?

An Ontario numbered corporation is from CA$599, including the $300 government fee. The minute book, books set-up and first-year planning are optional add-ons, quoted separately. Named, federal, professional and out-of-province corporations are quoted after a short call. The What it costs section lists the government fees separately.

Named or numbered?

A numbered corporation (for example, 1234567 Ontario Inc.) is faster and cheaper and can register a business name to trade under. A named corporation puts the name on the articles and protects it. Many small businesses start numbered and register the trade name.

How long does it take?

Ontario online filings are usually complete within a few business days of the name search; federal filings are similar. Opening the CRA accounts and the bank account adds a week or two.

Can I move my existing business into a corporation?

Yes. Equipment, inventory and goodwill can usually transfer on a tax-deferred basis with the right election, so the switch doesn't create a tax bill. We plan the transfer and file the election.

Which year-end should the corporation have?

We recommend December 31 for most owner-managed corporations. It lines up with your personal return, so salary, dividends, RRSP room and instalments are planned on one calendar.

Do you handle professional corporations?

Yes — medicine professional corporations and other regulated professions. The articles have to meet the regulator's requirements, and we prepare them. The Certificate of Authorization is a separate application you make to your College; we don't file it, but we give you the incorporation documents it requires.
