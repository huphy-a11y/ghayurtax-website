Should I incorporate?

Incorporation defers tax. It doesn't erase it.

An Ontario medicine professional corporation pays about 11.2% combined tax on its first $500,000 of practice income — the small business rate, after Ontario's cut to 2.2% on July 1, 2026. The top personal rate in Ontario is 53.53%. That gap, roughly 41 cents on every dollar you can leave in the corporation, is the whole reason to incorporate.

But the personal tax is deferred, not avoided. When you eventually pay yourself, you pay it. A physician who takes everything out every year ends up in roughly the same place as an unincorporated one, with more paperwork and a few thousand dollars a year in extra costs.

What incorporation gives you

  • Deferral on what you retain. Money left in the corporation is invested at the low rate, or used to smooth income across a parental leave, a sabbatical or retirement.
  • A choice of salary or dividends. Salary builds RRSP room and CPP; dividends don't, but avoid payroll costs. Most physicians use a mix that changes as income and plans change.
  • Some income splitting. Ontario lets a spouse, children and parents hold non-voting shares, but the tax on split income (TOSI) rules mean dividends to family members who don't work in the practice are generally taxed at the top rate. The exceptions — a spouse once you're 65, or a family member who works 20 or more hours a week in the practice — are real, but narrower than they were before 2018.
  • Room to add later. A corporate health spending account, and an individual pension plan when the numbers justify it.

What it doesn't do

  • Protect you from a malpractice claim — the professional liability stays with you and your CMPA coverage.
  • Reduce your total tax on its own.
  • Come free — expect a setup fee plus the government and CPSO Certificate of Authorization fees, then annual accounting, filing and CPSO costs.

When it makes sense

The test is simple: can you leave money in the corporation? If you can retain $40,000 to $50,000 a year or more after paying yourself what you actually spend, incorporation usually pays for itself quickly. If you spend everything you earn, wait — you'll get the cost without the benefit.

The other signs it's time: your billing is stable and self-employed (residents and salaried physicians can't run employment income through an MPC), you'd rather grow investments at 12% tax than 53%, and you can see yourself in practice for years.

One thing to plan for

Once the corporation earns more than $50,000 a year in passive investment income, the small business rate starts to shrink. It's a good problem — and one that's easy to manage if you see it coming.

If you have student loans

Pay down the debt, or defer the tax?

Loan payments come out of personal, after-tax dollars — the corporation can't pay them for you. So the real question is whether surplus income should clear the debt or stay in the corporation at 12.2%. The answer depends on which loans you have.

  • The federal portion of Canada Student Loans has been interest-free since April 2023. There is no return in rushing it. Pay it on schedule.
  • The Ontario portion of OSAP charges prime plus 1%, and the interest earns a non-refundable credit on your personal return. Worth paying steadily, not urgently.
  • A bank professional line of credit usually runs near prime, with no tax credit. High-rate debt is different from low-rate debt: clear anything expensive first. For debt near prime, the deferral is large enough that keeping the payments on schedule and retaining income in the corporation often comes out ahead — provided the retained money is actually invested rather than spent.
  • Don't fold government loans into a bank line without thinking about it: you'd give up the interest-free federal portion and the credit on the Ontario interest.

We run both paths — repay first, or incorporate and retain — with your actual income, debt and spending before you decide. It takes an hour and it's usually obvious once the numbers are on the page.

  1. Year one of practice: stay unincorporated

    Income is variable and you're learning what you actually spend. Build a cash cushion and clear any high-interest debt.

  2. Incorporate when you can retain $40–50K a year

    Pay yourself a salary that covers living costs and the loan payments. Leave the rest in the corporation.

  3. Keep the loans personal and on schedule

    Federal portion interest-free; claim the credit on Ontario and other government loan interest each year.

  4. Review every year

    As income rises, revisit the salary-and-dividend mix, RRSP room, and when the passive-income rules start to bite.

Common questions

What physicians ask us first.

I'm a resident. Should I incorporate now?

No. Residency income is employment income on a T4 and can't be run through a medical professional corporation. Incorporate once you're billing as self-employed — locums or your own practice — and can leave money in the corporation.

Can the corporation pay my student loans?

No. Student debt is personal. The corporation pays you, by salary or dividends, and you pay the loan. If the corporation paid it directly, the amount would be taxed to you as a shareholder benefit or treated as a shareholder loan.

Can my spouse own shares in my MPC?

Yes. Ontario allows a spouse, children and parents to hold non-voting shares in a medicine professional corporation. Whether it helps depends on the tax on split income rules: dividends to a spouse who doesn't work in the practice are generally taxed at the top rate until you turn 65. We plan it case by case.

Which year-end should I choose?

We usually recommend December 31. It lines the corporation's year up with your personal return, so salary, dividends, RRSP room and instalments are all planned on the same calendar and nothing falls between two periods. A different month can make sense in a few situations, and we'll tell you if yours is one of them.

How much does incorporation cost?

A fixed fee for the incorporation and setup, plus the government filing fee and the CPSO Certificate of Authorization fee, and then a few thousand dollars a year for the corporate return, financial statements, annual filings and CPSO renewal. Call or email and we'll give you the number for your situation.

Do you handle my personal return too?

Yes — we prefer to. The corporation and the family's returns are one plan, not two.
